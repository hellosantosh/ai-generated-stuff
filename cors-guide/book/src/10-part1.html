Part I

A Web Without Walls

Before a single CORS header makes sense, you need the thing it relaxes: the same-origin policy, the oldest and most important security boundary in your browser. This part tells the story of why it exists, what it guards, and the clever, dangerous hacks developers used before CORS arrived.

Chapter 1

The launch that went dark

Every developer meets CORS the same way: as a red line in the console, at the worst possible moment. This book starts there, too.

Harbor Pay · Launch night · 11:52 p.m.

You have been at Harbor Pay for six weeks. Tonight the new customer dashboard goes live: a fast single-page app at app.harborpay.example, talking to the company's API at api.harborpay.example. It worked perfectly on your laptop. It passed every test.

At 11:52 the deploy finishes. You open the dashboard. The balance card spins, and spins. You open DevTools and the console is a wall of red:

Access to fetch at 'https://api.harborpay.example/v1/balance' from origin
'https://app.harborpay.example' has been blocked by CORS policy: No
'Access-Control-Allow-Origin' header is present on the requested resource.

In the team channel, the suggestions arrive fast. “Just add Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *.” Then: “Star won't work with cookies. Make the server copy back whatever Origin it receives — that always works.” Someone pastes a one-line middleware snippet from a forum.

Your cursor hovers over the merge button. Then Ren, the security lead, joins the call. “Before anybody touches that header,” Ren says, “can someone tell me what it actually protects?”

Nobody answers. The launch waits.

The question this book answers

That silence is the reason this book exists. The error message tells you what is missing; it does not tell you what the missing thing means. The two “quick fixes” offered on the call make opposite trade-offs. The first shares public data with everyone, which is often fine. The second, copying back any Origin with cookies allowed, can hand every website on the internet the ability to read your signed-in customers' data. Both make the red line disappear. Neither is the right answer for a bank.

CORS in one sentence. Cross-Origin Resource Sharing is the protocol a server uses to tell the browser which other websites may read its responses — a controlled exception to a rule the browser otherwise enforces without mercy.

How this book is organized

We will follow the Harbor Pay team from that launch night to a security review three weeks later. Each chapter opens with a scene, then turns the scene into principles, diagrams, and a lab you can run on your own machine.

What you need

To read it

Comfort with HTTP requests and responses, and enough JavaScript to read a fetch() call. No security background is assumed.

To run the labs

Node.js 18 or later and any modern browser. The lab server has zero dependencies and prints its full source in Part V.

To use it at work

Chapter 20's checklist and reference configurations are written to be copied into a design review.

Three conventions

  • Story passages appear on a warm background, like the one on the previous page. Harbor Pay and its people are fictional; the technology and the incidents are real.
  • Example domains end in .example, a name reserved for documentation, so nothing in this book points at a real system.
  • Header names are case-insensitive in HTTP. The book writes them in their usual capitalization, Access-Control-Allow-Origin, and lowercase in code, where Node.js presents them that way.
Where the story goes. Tonight's launch is fixed in Chapter 9. The harder question Ren asked — what the header protects — takes until Chapter 13 to answer fully, and it changes how the whole team designs APIs.

Chapter 2

Why browsers built walls

Your browser is the most trusting program you run. It carries your identity to every site you are signed in to — automatically, on every request. That generosity is why the wall has to exist.

Launch night · 12:10 a.m.

Ren shares a screen with two browser tabs. One is Harbor Pay's dashboard, signed in. The other is a recipe blog. “Both of these pages run code in the same program, on the same machine, with the same cookies,” Ren says. “So why can't the recipe blog read your bank balance?”

Ambient authority: the browser's great convenience

When you sign in to a website, it usually hands your browser a session cookie. From then on, the browser attaches that cookie to every request it sends to that site — no matter which page, tab, or script caused the request. Security engineers call this ambient authority: your credentials are simply in the air, applied automatically.

It is what makes the web feel seamless. It is also a loaded gun. If any page could send a request to your bank and read the answer, then every page you visit could read your statements, your email, your medical portal — using your own cookies.

The rule arrives

JavaScript shipped in Netscape Navigator 2 in the mid-1990s, and within months the browser makers faced the problem in Figure 2.1. Their answer was the same-origin policy (SOP): a script may freely read data that comes from its own origin, and is cut off from data that comes from anywhere else. Every browser since has adopted it, and every later web feature — frames, XMLHttpRequest, fetch(), canvas, service workers — has had to answer the question “how does this respect the same-origin policy?”

The same-origin policy is not a feature you turn on. It is the default state of the web, and every cross-origin capability is an exception carved out of it.

Who enforces it, and against whom

Three facts about the same-origin policy explain almost every surprise you will meet with CORS:

1 · The browser enforces it

Not the server, not the network. A server cannot enforce the SOP, and tools like curl, Postman or a Node script ignore it entirely. This is why “it works in Postman” tells you nothing about CORS.

2 · It protects the user

The SOP protects the person at the keyboard from hostile pages misusing their signed-in sessions. It does not protect your server from anyone who can send HTTP requests directly.

3 · It is about reading

Browsers still send many cross-origin requests — images, forms, links. What the policy withholds is the ability of a script to read the response. Chapter 4 makes this precise.

Why this matters in 2026 more than in 1996

In the 1990s a website was mostly one server rendering HTML. Today a typical product is a constellation: a single-page app on one origin, APIs on others, an authentication provider, a CDN, analytics, payment widgets. Legitimate cross-origin reads are everywhere, which means exceptions to the same-origin policy are everywhere — and each one is a small security decision written in a response header.

The stakes. When a CORS exception is written too broadly, the same-origin policy stops protecting the users of that API. Nothing crashes and no alarm sounds. The wall simply has a door in it that nobody meant to install.

What exactly is an origin?

Three parts of a URL decide which side of the wall a page lives on. Get this definition exactly right and half of CORS debugging disappears.

Same origin, or not? Compared with https://app.harborpay.example

https://app.harborpay.example/settingsSameOnly the path differs
https://app.harborpay.example:443/Same443 is the default port for https

The most common surprise. Your app and your API on sibling subdomains of the same company domain are different origins. The same-origin policy does not care who owns them. That is exactly Harbor Pay's launch-night situation.

Origin is not “site”

Browsers use a second, coarser boundary called a site: the scheme plus the registrable domain — the part of the host just below a public suffix such as .com or .co.uk. The list of public suffixes is maintained as the Public Suffix List, which is why a.github.io and b.github.io are different sites even though they share a parent domain.

Two URLsSame origin?Same site?Which rules use it

other.example		NoNoDifferent in every sense.

Keep the two words apart. CORS is about origins. Cookie rules such as SameSite (Chapter 9) are about sites. Mixing them up produces bugs that appear in one environment and vanish in another.

The strange case of null

Some documents have no meaningful origin, and browsers give them an opaque origin, which is serialized in headers as the literal string null. You will see Origin: null from:

  • pages opened from the local disk (file:// URLs);
  • documents created from data: URLs;
  • sandboxed iframes (the sandbox attribute without allow-same-origin).

An opaque origin is never equal to any other origin, including another null. That makes null a description, not an identity — many unrelated documents all share the same string. Chapter 13 explains why a server must never treat it as a trusted caller.

How the browser tells the server

On cross-origin requests the browser adds an Origin request header naming the origin of the page that made the request. A page's script cannot forge it: it is one of the forbidden request headers that only the browser may set.

GET /v1/balance HTTP/1.1
Host: api.harborpay.example
Origin: https://app.harborpay.example     <- set by the browser, not by the page
Cookie: hp_session=…
Trust with care. The Origin header is reliable inside a browser; anyone using curl can type any value. Fine for CORS, which protects browser users — but never a substitute for authentication.

Chapter 4

What the wall blocks, and what it doesn't

The same-origin policy is famous and widely misunderstood. It does not stop your browser from talking to other origins. It stops pages from reading what comes back.

Why sending was never blocked

The web was built on links and forms that point at other sites. Blocking cross-origin sending would have broken the web of 1996, so it was never on the table. Instead, the rule became: you may cause a request; you may not read the result unless the other origin agrees. That compromise has two consequences you will meet again:

  • Side effects still happen. If a cross-origin request changes data on the server, it changes it whether or not the page can read the response. That is the root of cross-site request forgery, and the reason Chapter 16 insists CORS is not a CSRF defense.
  • Embedding can leak. Anything a browser will execute across origins — such as a script — must never contain private data. Chapter 5 shows what happened when sites forgot.
Remember this sentence. The same-origin policy governs reading, not sending. CORS lets a server say “this origin may read my response” — nothing more, nothing less.

Chapter 5

Life before CORS

For a decade, developers who needed cross-origin data had no official way to get it. So they improvised — and every improvisation taught the industry something about what a safe design would need.

mid-1990sJavaScript arrives in Netscape Navigator 2; the same-origin policy follows almost immediately.
1999–2005XMLHttpRequest spreads and “Ajax” gets its name in 2005 — but it is same-origin only.
2005–2010JSONP, server-side proxies and document.domain become the everyday workarounds.
2006–2007Researchers show that private data served as executable JavaScript can be read by other sites (“JavaScript hijacking”).
2009–2014Browsers implement CORS; it becomes a W3C Recommendation in January 2014 and later moves into the WHATWG Fetch Standard.

Workaround 1: JSONP

Scripts are allowed to load across origins (Figure 4.1, column 2). JSONP exploited that: instead of returning data, the server returned a script that called a function the page had defined, passing the data as its argument.

// the page defines a function, then adds a script tag pointing at the other origin
function showRates(data) { render(data); }
<script src="https://api.example/rates?callback=showRates"></script>

// the API responds with executable code rather than JSON
showRates({"USD": 1, "EUR": 0.92});

It worked everywhere, and it had two fatal properties. The page had to run code chosen by the other server with full privileges — total trust in the API. And the server had no way to choose who could read: any website could include the same script tag. JSONP was only ever safe for data you would publish to the whole internet.

Workaround 2: the server-side proxy

The page calls its own origin, and that server fetches from the other origin on its behalf. Because the browser only ever talks to one origin, the same-origin policy never triggers. Proxies remain a perfectly good pattern today (Chapter 20 recommends a form of it), but they add latency, cost, and a server that must be careful not to become an open relay for anyone's requests.

Workaround 3: document.domain

Two pages on sibling subdomains could both set document.domain to their shared parent and be treated as same-origin. It widened the wall for every page on those subdomains, not just the ones that needed it. Browsers have been retiring it; Chromium stopped honoring it by default in 2023. Treat it as history.

Workaround 4: postMessage

HTML5's window.postMessage lets windows on different origins exchange messages deliberately. It is still the right tool for cooperating documents, such as an embedded payment widget, and it is safe when the receiver checks event.origin against an exact expected value.

The lesson of JavaScript hijacking

Research · 2006–2007

When private data was served as code

In January 2006, security researcher Jeremiah Grossman described how a webmail service's contact list, delivered to its own pages as JavaScript, could be read by a page on a completely different site: the browser happily ran the cross-origin script, and the hostile page found a way to observe the data as it executed. The provider fixed the issue.

In 2007, researchers at Fortify Software (Brian Chess, Yekaterina Tsipenyuk O'Neil and Jacob West) published JavaScript Hijacking, showing the same weakness across many popular Ajax frameworks of the day and naming the class of bug.

What it taught: the “embed” column of Figure 4.1 is not a safe place for secrets. Responses containing private data should be plain data (JSON), never executable script, and should be readable only through a mechanism the server controls. That mechanism became CORS.
