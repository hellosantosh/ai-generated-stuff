2Why it matters

Three arguments are being made for Jev. One is economic, one is structural, and one is about what you can build when a decision costs a fraction of a cent. They are not equally strong.

2.1The economic argument

The headline numbers are striking and they are the vendor's own. TypeSafe publishes workflow evaluations at evals.typesafe.ai:

On TypeSafe's decision tasksJevGPT-6 Sol
Accuracy76.0%79.1%
Cost per call$0.0001$0.2152
Latency0.4 s34.3 s

From figures like these TypeSafe derives its headline multipliers: 193.6× faster and 444.6× cheaper at the extreme. The company is unusually candid about the caveat, noting the benchmarks were “designed by TypeSafe's own capabilities team” and that such multipliers represent “the higher end of real world gains”.

Read §7 before you quote any of these numbers

A TypeSafe employee measured a 15.9% speed improvement on a real pipeline when substituting one step with Jev, with cost down 30.1%. Component speed-ups do not become pipeline speed-ups when the other calls in the pipeline are unchanged. The number that decides an adoption is the pipeline number, and it is not the one in the headline.

2.2The structural argument

This one is more durable than the benchmarks, because it follows from the design rather than from a measurement.

  • The output cannot be malformed. Values outside the supplied schema are not representable, so the entire category of parse-retry-reprompt logic that surrounds LLM classification simply is not needed. TypeSafe quotes a 0% structured-output error rate; structurally, it is hard to see how it could be otherwise.
  • Confidence arrives with the answer. Not a self-reported “I am 90% sure” in prose, which is famously unreliable, but a probability per option. That makes abstention a first-class option: route the confident 95% automatically and send the rest to a human or a bigger model.
  • Latency is bounded and roughly flat in the number of questions. A single parallel pass means you can ask twelve questions about a document for approximately the price and time of asking one.
The honest framing of “cannot hallucinate”

It cannot hallucinate outside your schema. It can absolutely pick the wrong option inside it. “Structural correctness is not semantic correctness” is the most useful sentence written about this model so far, and it belongs in any summary you give a colleague who has only heard the marketing.

2.3The Jevons argument

The third argument is the one the model is named after. At roughly $0.0004 per decision against $0.03–$0.18 for a frontier model, some things stop being projects and become defaults:

  • Screening every fetched page for prompt injection, rather than the suspicious ones.
  • Judging every tool call a coding agent proposes, rather than sampling them.
  • Classifying an entire documentation site — TypeSafe demonstrates 500+ pages for under ten cents.
  • Reranking search results inside a request, where three seconds of LLM latency was previously prohibitive.

Whether that is transformative or merely convenient depends entirely on whether the decisions are good enough at that price, which is the question §7 is about. But the direction is real: TypeSafe notes the pricing “may be subsidized” and that it expects prices “to go down, not up”.
