How to set the screener's filters to find companies that are growing fast, are profitable or on their way to profitability, generate positive cash flow, and have strong analyst support. Includes why forward P/E matters and sample results from public data.

Most of the filters you need are in the Company Growth and Management & Growth panels. Analyst ratings are not in those top panels. Scroll further down the screener to the category named something like Analyst Opinions or Equity Summary Score.

The values below are reasonable starting points, not fixed rules. A screen narrows the list, so loosen or tighten the values based on how many stocks come back.

7How to use it

  1. Start small. Begin with the 8 core filters in the quick-start box. If you check everything at once, you'll probably get zero results.
  2. Adjust until you have 20–50 stocks. Too few: lower growth to 10% or market cap to $500M. Too many: raise growth to 20–25%.
  3. Research the list by hand. A screen only produces candidates. For each one, read the latest earnings report, the company's competitive advantages, how fast margins are improving, and how much the share count is being diluted (check the Shares Outstanding trend and stock-based compensation).
  4. Re-run the screen quarterly after earnings season, because growth figures change.

8Why P/E (Next Year's Estimate) matters for growth stocks

P/E (Next Year's Estimate), also called the forward P/E, is the current share price divided by the earnings per share (EPS) that analysts, on average, expect the company to earn in its next fiscal year:

Forward P/E = Share price ÷ Analysts' consensus EPS estimate for next year

The usual P/E (Price/TTM Earnings) divides by the last twelve months of earnings, which have already happened. The forward P/E divides by earnings that are expected. For a slow, steady company the two are close. For a growth company they can be very different, and the forward P/E is usually the more useful number.

1. The trailing P/E makes fast growers look too expensive

When earnings grow quickly, last year's earnings understate what the company earns now. Take a $100 stock that earned $2 per share last year and is expected to earn $3.33 next year. Its trailing P/E is 50, which looks expensive, but its forward P/E is 30. The gap between the two shows how much growth the market expects. The screen results in section 9 show this clearly:

HubSpot (HUBS)77.112.9Very large gap. Part of it is accounting (see limitations below)
Microsoft (MSFT)27.821.2Mature grower: a small gap

2. It identifies companies "on their way to profitability"

A company that lost money over the last year has no meaningful trailing P/E. If analysts expect a profit next year, its forward P/E is positive. That is why the screen uses Next Year Estimate > 0: it lets in companies that are just becoming profitable and keeps out companies with no profit in sight. Zeta Global (ZETA) in section 9 is an example: it has no trailing P/E because it was not profitable over the last twelve months, but its forward P/E is 25.1.

3. It is a guardrail against overpaying

Growth stocks often trade at high prices. Capping the forward P/E (at 60 in this screen) removes stocks priced so high that even strong growth may not justify the price. The PEG ratio takes this a step further by dividing the P/E by the expected growth rate, so a PEG under 2 means the price is reasonable for the growth expected.

4. It shows what the market is betting on

Trailing P/E ÷ forward P/E is roughly 1 + next year's expected earnings growth. Samsara's 248 ÷ 40 ≈ 6 means analysts expect earnings to rise about six-fold. That is only worth paying for if the company delivers, so the forward P/E also tells you what has to go right.

Limitations of the forward P/E
  • It is an estimate. Analysts are often too optimistic, and estimates get cut. Check whether estimates have been rising or falling over the last 90 days.
  • Estimates are usually "adjusted" EPS, which leaves out costs such as stock-based compensation. This is why some software companies (like HubSpot above) show a very large gap between trailing and forward P/E.
  • It covers only one year. For a five-year view, use it together with PEG and Forward Long Term EPS Growth (3–5 Yrs).
  • Compare within an industry. A forward P/E of 25 is cheap for fast-growing software and expensive for a utility.
  • Cyclical companies can be a trap. Miners and chipmakers show their lowest forward P/E at the peak of a cycle, when earnings are temporarily high.

What stands out

  • Technology dominates. 17 of the 32 are chip, hardware, software or internet companies, many of them tied to AI spending, which is where fast, profitable growth is concentrated in 2026. A portfolio built only from this list would be heavily concentrated in one theme.
  • The miners probably don't belong. Gold, silver and copper miners pass because metal prices are high, not because the businesses are structurally growing. Their earnings can fall sharply when prices drop. To exclude them, add a sector filter that removes Basic Materials.
  • Some estimates are extreme. Very low PEG ratios (SNDK 0.13, NVDA 0.22, AVGO 0.29) come from analysts projecting 50%+ yearly EPS growth, which rarely lasts five years. Treat these numbers with extra caution.
  • Well-known names are missing from the full screen. Companies such as Meta, Eli Lilly, ServiceNow and MercadoLibre pass the core filters but fail at least one of the stricter ones (for example PEG, debt, or the last quarter's earnings surprise). Failing a filter doesn't disqualify a company. It just means you should look at why it failed.
