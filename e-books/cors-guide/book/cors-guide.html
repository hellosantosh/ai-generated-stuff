A Web Without Walls

Before a single CORS header makes sense, you need the thing it relaxes: the same-origin policy, the oldest and most important security boundary in your browser. This part tells the story of why it exists, what it guards, and the clever, dangerous hacks developers used before CORS arrived.

Chapter 1

The launch that went dark

Every developer meets CORS the same way: as a red line in the console, at the worst possible moment. This book starts there, too.

Harbor Pay · Launch night · 11:52 p.m.

You have been at Harbor Pay for six weeks. Tonight the new customer dashboard goes live: a fast single-page app at app.harborpay.example, talking to the company's API at api.harborpay.example. It worked perfectly on your laptop. It passed every test.

At 11:52 the deploy finishes. You open the dashboard. The balance card spins, and spins. You open DevTools and the console is a wall of red:

Access to fetch at 'https://api.harborpay.example/v1/balance' from origin
'https://app.harborpay.example' has been blocked by CORS policy: No
'Access-Control-Allow-Origin' header is present on the requested resource.

In the team channel, the suggestions arrive fast. “Just add Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *.” Then: “Star won't work with cookies. Make the server copy back whatever Origin it receives — that always works.” Someone pastes a one-line middleware snippet from a forum.

Your cursor hovers over the merge button. Then Ren, the security lead, joins the call. “Before anybody touches that header,” Ren says, “can someone tell me what it actually protects?”

Nobody answers. The launch waits.

The question this book answers

That silence is the reason this book exists. The error message tells you what is missing; it does not tell you what the missing thing means. The two “quick fixes” offered on the call make opposite trade-offs. The first shares public data with everyone, which is often fine. The second, copying back any Origin with cookies allowed, can hand every website on the internet the ability to read your signed-in customers' data. Both make the red line disappear. Neither is the right answer for a bank.

CORS in one sentence. Cross-Origin Resource Sharing is the protocol a server uses to tell the browser which other websites may read its responses — a controlled exception to a rule the browser otherwise enforces without mercy.

Chapter 2

Why browsers built walls

Your browser is the most trusting program you run. It carries your identity to every site you are signed in to — automatically, on every request. That generosity is why the wall has to exist.

Launch night · 12:10 a.m.

Ren shares a screen with two browser tabs. One is Harbor Pay's dashboard, signed in. The other is a recipe blog. “Both of these pages run code in the same program, on the same machine, with the same cookies,” Ren says. “So why can't the recipe blog read your bank balance?”

Ambient authority: the browser's great convenience

When you sign in to a website, it usually hands your browser a session cookie. From then on, the browser attaches that cookie to every request it sends to that site — no matter which page, tab, or script caused the request. Security engineers call this ambient authority: your credentials are simply in the air, applied automatically.

It is what makes the web feel seamless. It is also a loaded gun. If any page could send a request to your bank and read the answer, then every page you visit could read your statements, your email, your medical portal — using your own cookies.

The rule arrives

JavaScript shipped in Netscape Navigator 2 in the mid-1990s, and within months the browser makers faced the problem in Figure 2.1. Their answer was the same-origin policy (SOP): a script may freely read data that comes from its own origin, and is cut off from data that comes from anywhere else. Every browser since has adopted it, and every later web feature — frames, XMLHttpRequest, fetch(), canvas, service workers — has had to answer the question “how does this respect the same-origin policy?”

The same-origin policy is not a feature you turn on. It is the default state of the web, and every cross-origin capability is an exception carved out of it.

Who enforces it, and against whom

Three facts about the same-origin policy explain almost every surprise you will meet with CORS:

1 · The browser enforces it

Not the server, not the network. A server cannot enforce the SOP, and tools like curl, Postman or a Node script ignore it entirely. This is why “it works in Postman” tells you nothing about CORS.

2 · It protects the user

The SOP protects the person at the keyboard from hostile pages misusing their signed-in sessions. It does not protect your server from anyone who can send HTTP requests directly.

3 · It is about reading

Browsers still send many cross-origin requests — images, forms, links. What the policy withholds is the ability of a script to read the response. Chapter 4 makes this precise.

Why this matters in 2026 more than in 1996

In the 1990s a website was mostly one server rendering HTML. Today a typical product is a constellation: a single-page app on one origin, APIs on others, an authentication provider, a CDN, analytics, payment widgets. Legitimate cross-origin reads are everywhere, which means exceptions to the same-origin policy are everywhere — and each one is a small security decision written in a response header.

The stakes. When a CORS exception is written too broadly, the same-origin policy stops protecting the users of that API. Nothing crashes and no alarm sounds. The wall simply has a door in it that nobody meant to install.

Origin is not “site”

Browsers use a second, coarser boundary called a site: the scheme plus the registrable domain — the part of the host just below a public suffix such as .com or .co.uk. The list of public suffixes is maintained as the Public Suffix List, which is why a.github.io and b.github.io are different sites even though they share a parent domain.

Two URLsSame origin?Same site?Which rules use it

Keep the two words apart. CORS is about origins. Cookie rules such as SameSite (Chapter 9) are about sites. Mixing them up produces bugs that appear in one environment and vanish in another.

The strange case of null

Some documents have no meaningful origin, and browsers give them an opaque origin, which is serialized in headers as the literal string null. You will see Origin: null from:

  • pages opened from the local disk (file:// URLs);
  • documents created from data: URLs;
  • sandboxed iframes (the sandbox attribute without allow-same-origin).

An opaque origin is never equal to any other origin, including another null. That makes null a description, not an identity — many unrelated documents all share the same string. Chapter 13 explains why a server must never treat it as a trusted caller.

How the browser tells the server

On cross-origin requests the browser adds an Origin request header naming the origin of the page that made the request. A page's script cannot forge it: it is one of the forbidden request headers that only the browser may set.

GET /v1/balance HTTP/1.1
Host: api.harborpay.example
Origin: https://app.harborpay.example     <- set by the browser, not by the page
Cookie: hp_session=…
Trust with care. The Origin header is reliable inside a browser; anyone using curl can type any value. Fine for CORS, which protects browser users — but never a substitute for authentication.

Chapter 4

What the wall blocks, and what it doesn't

The same-origin policy is famous and widely misunderstood. It does not stop your browser from talking to other origins. It stops pages from reading what comes back.

Why sending was never blocked

The web was built on links and forms that point at other sites. Blocking cross-origin sending would have broken the web of 1996, so it was never on the table. Instead, the rule became: you may cause a request; you may not read the result unless the other origin agrees. That compromise has two consequences you will meet again:

  • Side effects still happen. If a cross-origin request changes data on the server, it changes it whether or not the page can read the response. That is the root of cross-site request forgery, and the reason Chapter 16 insists CORS is not a CSRF defense.
  • Embedding can leak. Anything a browser will execute across origins — such as a script — must never contain private data. Chapter 5 shows what happened when sites forgot.
Remember this sentence. The same-origin policy governs reading, not sending. CORS lets a server say “this origin may read my response” — nothing more, nothing less.

Chapter 5

Life before CORS

For a decade, developers who needed cross-origin data had no official way to get it. So they improvised — and every improvisation taught the industry something about what a safe design would need.

mid-1990sJavaScript arrives in Netscape Navigator 2; the same-origin policy follows almost immediately.
1999–2005XMLHttpRequest spreads and “Ajax” gets its name in 2005 — but it is same-origin only.
2005–2010JSONP, server-side proxies and document.domain become the everyday workarounds.
2006–2007Researchers show that private data served as executable JavaScript can be read by other sites (“JavaScript hijacking”).
2009–2014Browsers implement CORS; it becomes a W3C Recommendation in January 2014 and later moves into the WHATWG Fetch Standard.

Workaround 1: JSONP

Scripts are allowed to load across origins (Figure 4.1, column 2). JSONP exploited that: instead of returning data, the server returned a script that called a function the page had defined, passing the data as its argument.

// the page defines a function, then adds a script tag pointing at the other origin
function showRates(data) { render(data); }
<script src="https://api.example/rates?callback=showRates"></script>

// the API responds with executable code rather than JSON
showRates({"USD": 1, "EUR": 0.92});

It worked everywhere, and it had two fatal properties. The page had to run code chosen by the other server with full privileges — total trust in the API. And the server had no way to choose who could read: any website could include the same script tag. JSONP was only ever safe for data you would publish to the whole internet.

Workaround 2: the server-side proxy

The page calls its own origin, and that server fetches from the other origin on its behalf. Because the browser only ever talks to one origin, the same-origin policy never triggers. Proxies remain a perfectly good pattern today (Chapter 20 recommends a form of it), but they add latency, cost, and a server that must be careful not to become an open relay for anyone's requests.

Workaround 3: document.domain

Two pages on sibling subdomains could both set document.domain to their shared parent and be treated as same-origin. It widened the wall for every page on those subdomains, not just the ones that needed it. Browsers have been retiring it; Chromium stopped honoring it by default in 2023. Treat it as history.

Workaround 4: postMessage

HTML5's window.postMessage lets windows on different origins exchange messages deliberately. It is still the right tool for cooperating documents, such as an embedded payment widget, and it is safe when the receiver checks event.origin against an exact expected value.

The lesson of JavaScript hijacking

Research · 2006–2007

When private data was served as code

In January 2006, security researcher Jeremiah Grossman described how a webmail service's contact list, delivered to its own pages as JavaScript, could be read by a page on a completely different site: the browser happily ran the cross-origin script, and the hostile page found a way to observe the data as it executed. The provider fixed the issue.

In 2007, researchers at Fortify Software (Brian Chess, Yekaterina Tsipenyuk O'Neil and Jacob West) published JavaScript Hijacking, showing the same weakness across many popular Ajax frameworks of the day and naming the class of bug.

What it taught: the “embed” column of Figure 4.1 is not a safe place for secrets. Responses containing private data should be plain data (JSON), never executable script, and should be readable only through a mechanism the server controls. That mechanism became CORS.

Chapter 6

Enter CORS

CORS does not tear down the wall. It gives the server a way to hand the browser a permission slip: “responses from me may be read by this origin.”

The core idea, in three sentences

  1. On every cross-origin request, the browser sends an Origin header naming the page's origin.
  2. The server answers normally, and adds Access-Control-Allow-Origin naming the origin it is willing to share with — or * for anyone.
  3. The browser compares the two. If they match, the page may read the response; if not, the page gets an error and never sees the body.
Opt-in by design. A server that has never heard of CORS sends no Access-Control-* headers, so its responses stay unreadable to other origins — exactly as they were before CORS existed. Nothing old became less safe when CORS arrived.

Where CORS lives today

CORS became a W3C Recommendation in January 2014; in 2017 the W3C marked it obsolete because the living definition had moved into the WHATWG Fetch Standard, today's authority for every edge case. The origin itself is defined in RFC 6454 (2011).

Three myths to drop now

“CORS protects my API.”

It protects browser users of your API. Scripts and command-line tools ignore it; your API still needs real authorization.

“A CORS error means the request failed.”

Often the request reached the server and succeeded. The browser only refused to show the page the response. Lab 3 proves it.

“I fix CORS in the front end.”

Permission comes from the server's response headers. No option in fetch() can grant a page access the server didn't give.

Chapter 7

Simple requests

Some cross-origin requests go straight to the server with no questions asked. The reason is historical, and once you see it, the rules stop looking arbitrary.

The logic: nothing a form couldn't already do

Long before CORS, any page could build an HTML form that submitted a GET or a POST to any server, with a handful of content types. Servers already had to live with those requests arriving from anywhere. So CORS reasoned: if a script sends a request that a form could have sent, letting it through creates no new danger. Such a request is called simple (the Fetch Standard's term is a request that needs no CORS-preflight).

Designing with simple requests in mind

Knowing the rule lets you choose deliberately. Read-only public endpoints can stay simple and fast. Endpoints that change data should require something a form cannot send — a JSON body or a custom header — so that browsers must preflight them, and your policy gets a say before anything happens. The lab server's notes endpoint does exactly this: it refuses any request that is not application/json.

Chapter 8

The preflight

For any request a form couldn't send, the browser asks first. The question is an OPTIONS request, and nothing else is sent until the server says yes.

What the browser checks in the preflight answer

  • The status is a success (2xx). A redirect or a 401 fails the preflight.
  • Access-Control-Allow-Origin matches the page's origin (or is * when no credentials are involved).
  • The requested method appears in Access-Control-Allow-Methods. Simple methods are always allowed.
  • Every header in Access-Control-Request-Headers appears in Access-Control-Allow-Headers.
Chapter 8 · The preflight20
Chapter 8 · continued

Why the preflight carries no credentials

The preflight is sent without cookies and without a body, even when the real request will include them. It is a question about policy (“would you accept this kind of request from this origin?”), not about any user. Two practical consequences follow:

  • Don't require authentication on OPTIONS. An API gateway that answers unauthenticated preflights with 401 breaks every non-simple request from the browser. Answer the preflight from policy alone, then authenticate the actual request.
  • A preflight never performs the action. Your handler for the real PUT should not run for an OPTIONS request. Most frameworks route preflights to CORS middleware automatically; hand-written servers must do it themselves, as the lab server's applyCors function does.

Wildcards in a preflight answer

The Fetch Standard allows * in Access-Control-Allow-Methods and Access-Control-Allow-Headers, meaning “any” — but only for requests without credentials. With credentials, * is treated as a literal name that matches nothing useful. One more exception: the wildcard in Allow-Headers never covers Authorization, which must always be listed by name. Listing exactly what you use is clearer anyway, and it documents your API's contract.

Caching the answer

A preflight costs a full round trip before every non-simple request. Access-Control-Max-Age lets the browser remember a successful answer, keyed on the origin, the URL, and the method or header being allowed.

SituationEffective cache lifetime
No Max-Age header5 seconds (the Fetch Standard's default)
Max-Age: 60010 minutes
Max-Age: 86400Capped by the browser: Chromium limits it to 2 hours, Firefox to 24 hours

A value of a few minutes to an hour is a sensible default. Long caches mean a policy change takes that long to reach browsers that already asked; short ones cost latency. Lab 7 lets you count preflights as you change the value.

Performance tip. Preflights are cached per URL, so an API with identifiers in its paths pays for a fresh preflight on every new URL. Serving the API from the app's own origin (Chapter 20) removes the cost entirely.

Chapter 9

Credentials and cookies

Reading public data across origins is easy. Reading data that belongs to a signed-in user is where CORS gets strict — deliberately, because that is where the danger is.

Two opt-ins, from two different parties

By default, fetch() sends cookies only to the page's own origin. For a cross-origin request to carry the user's cookies and have its response readable, both sides must opt in:

The rules with credentials

HeaderWith credentials
Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *Rejected must be the exact origin
Access-Control-Allow-CredentialsRequired exactly the string true
Allow-Methods / Allow-Headers: *No wildcard list the names
Access-Control-Expose-Headers: *No wildcard list the names

Why “*” and credentials may never meet

A * says “this response is fit for anyone.” That is safe only for a response that is the same for everyone. A response computed from a user's cookies is, by definition, not for anyone — it is for that user, in pages that the server trusts. The browser enforces the distinction so that a single careless header cannot publish every signed-in user's data to every website. Chapter 13 shows the tempting workaround that defeats this protection, and why it must be avoided.

Chapter 9 · Credentials and cookies22
Chapter 9 · continued

Cookies have rules of their own

CORS decides whether a page may read a credentialed response. Whether the cookie is attached in the first place is decided separately, by cookie attributes and browser privacy settings:

  • SameSite limits cookies on cross-site requests (Chapter 3's “site,” not origin). Strict never sends them cross-site, Lax sends them only on top-level navigations such as following a link, and None sends them everywhere but requires Secure. Chrome treats a cookie without the attribute as Lax.
  • Third-party cookie restrictions in Safari and Firefox block or partition cookies for cross-site requests by default, and other browsers let users choose. If your app and API are on unrelated sites, credentialed CORS may work in one browser and silently lose its cookie in another.

Harbor Pay is lucky: app.harborpay.example and api.harborpay.example are the same site, so a SameSite=Lax session cookie is attached to the dashboard's credentialed requests. If your front end and API live on different registrable domains, prefer serving the API from the app's own origin (Chapter 20), or use tokens in an Authorization header instead of cookies.

Chapter 10

Reading the response

Even when CORS allows a response, the page does not see all of it. And when CORS refuses, the page is told almost nothing — on purpose.

Only some headers are visible

A script reading a cross-origin response can see the body and the status, but only a small set of CORS-safelisted response headers:

Cache-Control · Content-Language · Content-Length · Content-Type · Expires · Last-Modified · Pragma

Anything else — a request ID, a rate-limit counter, a pagination link — is hidden from res.headers.get() unless the server lists it:

Access-Control-Expose-Headers: X-Request-Id, X-RateLimit-Remaining, Link

DevTools will still show the header in the Network tab, which makes this one of the most confusing CORS behaviors: you can see it, but your code gets null. Lab 5 reproduces exactly that.

Opaque responses and mode: 'no-cors'

Developers fighting a CORS error sometimes discover fetch(url, {mode: 'no-cors'}) and think the problem is solved, because the error disappears. It isn't. A no-cors request returns an opaque response: status 0, no headers, and an empty body. The browser may use it (a service worker can cache it for an image tag), but your script can never read it. no-cors is how the browser keeps old embedding behavior working — not a way around the same-origin policy.

Why the error says so little

When a CORS check fails, fetch() rejects with a generic error — TypeError: Failed to fetch in Chromium, NetworkError when attempting to fetch resource in Firefox, Load failed in Safari. The script cannot learn the status code, whether the server was reachable, or which header was wrong.

That vagueness is a security feature. If a page could distinguish “server said 404” from “server said 200 but CORS refused,” it could probe other origins — including servers on your private network — and learn about them without ever being allowed to read them. The detailed explanation is written only to the DevTools console, for the human at the keyboard.

Debugging rule of thumb. Never debug CORS from your application's error handler. Open the Console for the reason and the Network tab for the raw headers; the next chapter shows how to read them.

Four traps that waste an afternoon

Chapter 12

Why CORS is a security priority

A CORS policy is often the only thing standing between a page your users happen to visit and the data your API holds about them. That makes a single response header one of the most consequential lines in your codebase.

Three weeks later · Security review, 10:00 a.m.

Six reasons it deserves attention

1 · It guards the user's own session

Your authentication works perfectly and still doesn't help: the request really is from the signed-in user's browser, carrying their real cookie. The only question is which page gets to read the answer. That question is CORS.

2 · APIs are the product now

Single-page apps, mobile back ends and partner integrations put account data, payment details and personal records behind JSON endpoints. Those are exactly the responses CORS decides about.

3 · Mistakes are silent

A too-strict policy produces red errors and angry tickets. A too-loose policy produces nothing at all — every test passes. The feedback loop rewards loosening and never punishes it.

4 · The fix is tempting and tiny

Under launch pressure, the difference between a correct policy and a dangerous one is a few characters. Forums are full of snippets that make errors disappear by trusting everyone.

5 · Reach is multiplied

A policy bug is not one user's problem. It affects every signed-in user of that API, from every browser, until someone notices.

6 · Standards bodies track it

The weakness has its own entries — CWE-942 (Permissive Cross-domain Policy with Untrusted Domains) and CWE-346 (Origin Validation Error) — and the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide has a dedicated test, WSTG-CLNT-07.

What is actually at stake

If a policy lets an untrusted origin read credentialed responses, any page a signed-in customer visits can see what that customer sees in your app, through their own session. No password is stolen and no server is breached, so the logs show only normal requests.

The one-line summary. CORS misconfiguration turns your users' browsers against them. It is a privacy and data-exposure problem first, and its blast radius is every signed-in user.

Chapter 12 · continued

Where risk actually lives

Not every permissive header is a problem. Risk depends on two questions: does the response depend on who is asking (cookies, network location, or other ambient credentials), and whom does the policy allow to read it?

“But we have authentication”

This is the most common objection in a review, and it misses the point. Authentication answers “is this request from Alice?” In the danger zone the answer is honestly yes — Alice's browser sent it, with Alice's cookie. What went wrong is that a page Alice didn't intend to trust got to read the reply. Token-based APIs that never use cookies are far less exposed for the same reason: the browser does not attach a bearer token automatically, so there is no ambient credential to misuse.

What a good review asks

  1. Which responses from this service depend on cookies, sessions or network location?
  2. For those responses, exactly which origins can read them, and who approved each one?
  3. How is the Origin header compared — whole-string equality, or something cleverer (and therefore riskier)?
  4. Are the pages on every trusted origin themselves well defended against script injection?
  5. Do all responses, including errors and cached ones, carry the right headers and Vary: Origin?

Chapter 13

Configuration mistakes that matter

Almost every serious CORS problem is one of six patterns. Each one looks reasonable in a code review, and each one has a simple, boring fix.

Mistake 1 · Echoing any origin, with credentials

“Copy back whatever Origin arrives”

The server reads the request's Origin and repeats it in Access-Control-Allow-Origin, together with Allow-Credentials: true. It always “works,” which is why it spreads. It is also exactly the combination the browser's *-with-credentials rule exists to prevent: every origin on the internet is now trusted with your users' sessions. Watch for framework settings with friendly names — in some popular middleware, setting the origin option to true means “reflect the request's origin.”

Fix: compare the origin against an explicit list and repeat it only on an exact match. If the data is truly public, use * without credentials.
Mistake 2 · Loose matching

Prefixes, suffixes, substrings and hand-written patterns

Checks like “the origin contains our domain,” “ends with our domain,” or a regular expression with an unescaped dot or a missing anchor all accept more than their authors intended — including hostnames that someone else can register. The researchers in Chapter 15 found this class of bug to be widespread.

Fix: don't pattern-match origins. Keep a list of complete origin strings (scheme, host and port) and use exact equality. If you must support many subdomains, parse the origin with a URL parser and compare the parsed hostname against an exact set.
Mistake 3 · Trusting null

Allowing Origin: null “for local testing”

Developers add null to an allowlist so that a page opened from disk can call the API. But null is shared by every sandboxed iframe, every data: document and every local file on every machine (Chapter 3). Trusting it trusts all of them at once.

Fix: never allowlist null. Serve test pages from a real local origin such as http://localhost:8080, and keep development origins out of production configuration.

Chapter 13 · continued
Mistake 4 · Trusting plain http

Allowing http://app.harborpay.example as well as https

An http origin is only as trustworthy as every network between the user and the server. On a hostile Wi-Fi network, content served over plain HTTP can be altered in transit, so trusting the http version of your own site extends your API's trust to whoever controls that network.

Fix: allow only https origins in production, and use HSTS so that your sites are never loaded over plain HTTP in the first place.
Mistake 5 · Trusting every subdomain

“Anything under harborpay.example is ours”

A company domain accumulates marketing microsites, old campaign pages, third-party-hosted help centers and forgotten staging servers. Trusting all of them means your most sensitive API is only as safe as your least-maintained subdomain — including any with a cross-site scripting bug or a dangling DNS record pointing at a service someone else can claim.

Fix: list the specific origins that need access. Treat adding one as a security decision, and remove entries when the site that needed them is retired.
Mistake 6 · Network location as a credential

Allow-Origin: * on an internal API

“It's only reachable from the office network, so * is fine.” But the code that makes the request runs inside an employee's browser, which is on the office network. A wildcard on an unauthenticated intranet API lets any page an employee visits read it. Local services on localhost have the same problem (Chapter 18).

Fix: require real authentication on internal services, and never combine “trusted because of where it is” with *.

Plus one that breaks things before it breaks security

A dynamic policy (one that names different origins for different requests) without Vary: Origin lets caches serve one origin's permission to another. Chapter 17 covers it in depth; the fix is one header, on every response.

The pattern behind all six. Every mistake here replaces a short, explicit list of trusted origins with a rule that trusts a category — “whoever asks,” “anything that looks like us,” “anything local.” Categories grow without anyone deciding. Lists only grow when someone does.

How to read the diagrams

Red dashed arrows are steps an attacker can cause or influence — usually just by getting a signed-in user to open a web page.

Pattern 1 · The borrowed session

A hostile page reads your API through the user's own session

What happens. Nothing is stolen from the server and no password is guessed. The API does what it always does — answers the user's browser — and its CORS policy tells the browser that the untrusted page may read the answer.

Warning signs. Credentialed responses whose Access-Control-Allow-Origin names an origin you never approved; Origin values in your logs that no team recognizes.

Break the chain. An exact allowlist (Chapter 13), no credentials where the data isn't personal, and cookies with SameSite so the session isn't attached cross-site in the first place.

Research · October 2016 · OWASP AppSec USA

“Exploiting CORS Misconfigurations for Bitcoins and Bounties”

James Kettle of PortSwigger surveyed how real websites validated the Origin header and found that many used simple string operations — reflection, prefix and suffix checks — rather than exact comparison. Among the findings he described were cryptocurrency exchanges whose CORS policies could have let another website read sensitive account data from signed-in users; the issues were reported to the affected organizations. Research · August 2018 · USENIX Security Symposium

“We Still Don't Have Secure Cross-Domain Requests: an Empirical Study of CORS”

Jianjun Chen and colleagues measured CORS deployments across a large set of popular websites. They found insecure misconfigurations to be common, identified weaknesses in how CORS interacts with other web mechanisms, and noted that many sites whose policies varied by origin failed to send Vary: Origin. The authors released an open-source scanner, CORScanner, and proposed clarifications, some of which were incorporated into the specification.

Lesson: CORS mistakes are not rare accidents but a systematic pattern, driven by an inexpressive policy language and poor developer understanding. Treat CORS as something to test, not assume.
Web security history · October 2005

The Samy worm

A cross-site scripting flaw on MySpace let a user named Samy Kamkar put a script in a profile page. CORS is not CSRF protection

CORS decides who may read a response. Cross-site request forgery is about requests that change something. Confusing the two leaves real holes.

The gap, stated plainly

Simple requests (Chapter 7) reach the server before any CORS check, often with the user's cookies. If one moves money or deletes a record, the server does it; the browser then hides the response, but the damage is done. That is cross-site request forgery (CSRF), and no CORS header prevents it.

Require a non-simple requestAccept state changes only as strictly checked application/json or with a custom header. Forms cannot send those, and scripts must pass your preflight.

Chapter 17

Caches and Vary: Origin

The bug that makes CORS “work sometimes” is usually not in your CORS code at all. It is in a cache that doesn't know your answer depends on who asked.

How a correct policy goes wrong

Suppose Harbor Pay allows two origins, the dashboard and a partner portal. The API names whichever one is asking — a correct policy. Now put a CDN or reverse-proxy cache in front of it, keyed only on the URL:

The fix: tell every cache what the answer depends on

Vary: Origin

Vary lists the request headers a response depends on. With Vary: Origin, a cache stores a separate copy per origin, and each origin gets its own correct answer. The rules are short:

  • Send Vary: Origin whenever Access-Control-Allow-Origin can differ between requests — that is, on every response from a dynamic policy, including the ones where you send no CORS headers at all.
  • A fixed Access-Control-Allow-Origin: * does not vary, so it doesn't need it.
  • If your response already varies on other headers, combine them: Vary: Origin, Accept-Encoding.
Why it is a security issue too. The same mechanism can serve a response containing one user's data, or one origin's permission, to a different requester. Caches in front of personalized, credentialed responses need Cache-Control: private (or no-store) as well as correct Vary. The 2018 study in Chapter 15 found missing Vary: Origin to be widespread.

Chapter 18

The local network

Your browser sits on two networks at once: the public internet, and the private one around you — your router, your printer, services on localhost. For most of the web's history, public pages could send requests to both.

Why the local network is special

Devices and apps on private networks were often built on the assumption that “only people on my network can reach me.” But every web page you open runs inside your browser, which is on your network. The Zoom and Transmission cases in Chapter 15 both followed from this. CORS helps only partly: it stops a public page from reading a local service's responses, but simple requests still arrive, and a local service that acts on them can be driven from any website.

What browsers did about it

Chromium first proposed Private Network Access: before a public page could reach a private address, the browser would send a special preflight (Access-Control-Request-Private-Network: true) and require the device to opt in. Deployment revealed compatibility problems, and the design evolved into Local Network Access. Beginning with Chrome 142 in October 2025, a public site that tries to reach the local network or loopback triggers a permission prompt: the user, not the device, decides whether that site may talk to local devices. Other browsers have been evaluating similar protections; check current documentation for each.

What this means for you. If your product legitimately talks to a local device from a web page (a printer setup page, a hardware wallet bridge, a developer tool), expect a permission prompt in Chromium browsers, explain it to your users, and never rely on the prompt as your only defense.

The same-origin policy compares names. DNS decides what machine a name refers to, and a domain's owner can change that answer at any time, even to a private address. That lets a hostname that began as an ordinary public site later point at 127.0.0.1, while the browser still treats both as one origin. This is DNS rebinding, the issue behind the Transmission fix in Chapter 15. Browser-level defenses such as Local Network Access help, but the reliable fixes live in the local service itself.

Beyond CORS

CORS controls what a page may read through fetch(). A newer family of headers controls everything else: what may embed you, what you may embed, and who may share your process.

The turning point: Spectre

In January 2018, researchers disclosed Spectre, a class of CPU vulnerabilities that let code infer data from memory it was never allowed to read. For browsers the implication was stark: if a cross-origin resource was loaded into the same process as a hostile page — even as an “embedded” image or script it could not officially read — its bytes might be exposed. Browsers responded with Site Isolation (separate processes per site) and by blocking sensitive responses from ever reaching the wrong process: first Cross-Origin Read Blocking (CORB), now specified as Opaque Response Blocking (ORB) in the Fetch Standard. Servers got new headers to state their intentions explicitly.

Cross-origin isolation

A page served with both Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy: same-origin and Cross-Origin-Embedder-Policy: require-corp (or credentialless) becomes cross-origin isolated: self.crossOriginIsolated is true. Browsers then unlock powerful features they otherwise restrict, such as SharedArrayBuffer and more precise timers, because nothing from another origin can end up in the page's process without having agreed to it.

How CORS fits. Under COEP: require-corp, a cross-origin resource is accepted if it either sends a suitable CORP header or is fetched in CORS mode and passes the CORS check. CORS and CORP are the two ways a resource can say “yes, you may have me.”

A resource isolation policy

With these headers, a server can refuse unexpected cross-site traffic before it reaches any business logic — a strong complement to CORS (which governs reading) and to CSRF defenses (which govern writing):

// Allow same-origin and same-site requests, browser navigations, and old browsers
// that don't send the header. Refuse everything else from other sites.
function allowRequest(req) {
  const site = req.headers['sec-fetch-site'];
  if (!site) return true;                               // older browser
  if (['same-origin', 'same-site', 'none'].includes(site)) return true;
  if (req.headers['sec-fetch-mode'] === 'navigate' && req.method === 'GET') return true;
  return isPublicEndpoint(req.url);                     // explicit exceptions
}

Cross-site leaks: the frontier

Even when a page cannot read a response, it can sometimes observe side effects: how long a load took, whether a frame loaded, how many windows a site opened. The research community calls these cross-site leaks (XS-Leaks). They are the reason browsers keep adding isolation features and keep CORS errors deliberately vague (Chapter 10). The defenses are the ones in this chapter: SameSite cookies, Fetch Metadata checks, COOP, and CORP.

For APIs

Precise CORS allowlist, Vary: Origin, Fetch Metadata policy, CORP: same-site on private JSON.

For apps

COOP: same-origin, a strict content security policy, and SameSite session cookies.

For public assets

Allow-Origin: * without credentials, and CORP: cross-origin so isolated pages can use them.

Designing a secure CORS policy

The safest CORS policy is the one you never need. The next safest is short, explicit, and boring. Here is how to choose, and how to write it.

Three architectures

Rules for a credentialed allowlist

  1. Exact strings only. Full origins (https://host, plus port if not default), compared with equality. No substring, prefix, suffix or regular-expression checks.
  2. Never null, never http in production, and no development origins in production configuration.
  3. Headers only on a match. Unknown origins get no Access-Control-* headers at all — not an error message, not a default origin.
  4. Vary: Origin on every response the policy touches, allowed or not.
  5. Minimal methods and headers, listed by name; Max-Age of minutes to an hour.
  6. Preflights answered before authentication, and never running business logic.
  7. Expose only what the client needs in Access-Control-Expose-Headers.
  8. Configured in one place — app or gateway, not both.

The design-review checklist

  • ☐ Could this be same-origin instead (architecture A)?
  • ☐ Does any response depend on cookies or network location?
  • ☐ If * is used, are responses identical for every caller?
  • ☐ Is every allowed origin a full, exact https string?
  • ☐ No null, no http, no dev origins in production?
  • ☐ Does every trusted origin have XSS defenses and a named owner?
  • ☐ Vary: Origin on every response of a dynamic policy?
  • ☐ Preflights answered before authentication, no side effects?
  • ☐ Methods, headers and exposed headers listed minimally, by name?
  • ☐ CORS headers present on error responses too?
  • ☐ Configured in exactly one layer?
  • ☐ State-changing endpoints protected against CSRF (Chapter 16)?
  • ☐ Local services validate Host and authenticate (Chapter 18)?
  • ☐ Automated tests cover allowed and refused origins?
If you remember one design rule. Let the browser say no by default, and make every yes a named, reviewed, tested decision. That is what Access-Control-Allow-Origin was designed to express.

Epilogue: the review

Harbor Pay · Six weeks after launch

The ten tickets are closed. The five services that checked whether an origin ended with the company domain now compare against exact lists. On a Friday afternoon, a new engineer posts in the team channel: “Getting a CORS error on the partner portal — can I just set Allow-Origin to star?”

You smile, and start typing. “Before anybody touches that header,” you write, “can you tell me what it actually protects?”

Seven things to carry with you

  1. The same-origin policy is the default; CORS is a set of exceptions to it, granted by servers and enforced by browsers.
  2. It governs reading, not sending. Simple requests reach the server regardless; protect state changes separately.
  3. An origin is scheme, host and port. Sibling subdomains and different ports are different origins.
  4. Credentials raise the bar: an exact origin, Allow-Credentials: true, and no wildcards — and that bar exists for good reason.
  5. Compare origins with exact equality against a reviewed list. Every clever rule is a future incident.
  6. Send Vary: Origin whenever the answer depends on the asker.
  7. The best CORS policy is often none at all: serve the API from the app's origin.

Glossary

Every term in one place, with the chapter that explains it.

Ambient authority

Credentials such as cookies that the browser attaches automatically, whoever caused the request. (Ch. 2)

Access-Control-Allow-Origin

The response header naming the one origin, or *, allowed to read the response. (Ch. 6)

CORP, COEP, COOP

Cross-Origin Resource, Embedder and Opener Policies: headers controlling embedding and process isolation. (Ch. 19)

CORS

Cross-Origin Resource Sharing: the protocol by which a server lets the browser share its responses with named other origins. (Ch. 6)

CORS-safelisted

The request headers, content types and response headers that need no special permission. (Ch. 7, 10)

Credentials

Cookies, HTTP authentication and TLS client certificates. Sent cross-origin only with credentials: 'include'. (Ch. 9)

Cross-origin isolation

The state of a page served with COOP and COEP, which unlocks features such as SharedArrayBuffer. (Ch. 19)

CSRF

Cross-site request forgery: causing a user's browser to send a state-changing request they didn't intend. Not prevented by CORS. (Ch. 16)

DNS rebinding

Changing what a hostname resolves to, so a browser treats a local address as the same origin as a public site. (Ch. 15, 18)

Fetch Metadata

The Sec-Fetch-* request headers describing how a request came about. (Ch. 19)

Fetch Standard

The WHATWG living standard that defines fetching, including CORS. The authoritative source. (Ch. 6)

JSONP

A pre-CORS technique that delivered data as an executable script. Obsolete. (Ch. 5)

Local Network Access

Chromium's permission-based protection for requests from public sites to local and loopback addresses. (Ch. 18)

Opaque origin

An origin with no identity, serialized as null; never equal to any other origin. (Ch. 3)

Opaque response

The result of a no-cors fetch: usable by the browser, unreadable by script. (Ch. 10)

Origin

The triple of scheme, host and port that the same-origin policy compares. (Ch. 3)

ORB / CORB

Opaque (formerly Cross-Origin) Read Blocking: stops sensitive responses from entering another site's process. (Ch. 19)

Preflight

The OPTIONS request a browser sends to ask permission before a non-simple request. (Ch. 8)

Public Suffix List

The list of suffixes, like .com or .github.io, under which domains are registered; it defines sites. (Ch. 3)

Same-origin policy

The browser rule that a page may read only responses from its own origin, unless CORS permits otherwise. (Ch. 2, 4)

SameSite

A cookie attribute that limits sending the cookie on cross-site requests. (Ch. 9, 16)

Simple request

A request a form could send, which needs no preflight. (Ch. 7)

Site

Scheme plus registrable domain. Coarser than an origin; used by cookies. (Ch. 3)

Vary

The response header listing the request headers a response depends on; Vary: Origin keeps caches correct. (Ch. 17)

XS-Leaks

Cross-site leaks: information inferred from side effects of cross-origin loads. (Ch. 19)
