Chapter 11

Why a catalog needs an ontology

It sounds like an academic luxury until the catalog passes about a dozen tools. Then three problems arrive at once.

Nobody designs a tool catalog badly on purpose. What happens is that the first five tools are written by one person in a week, the next fifteen by four people over a quarter, and the forty after that by teams who have read the first twenty and drawn their own conclusions. By then the catalog has three diseases, and they are not independent.

The three problems

One: the names stop agreeing

Here is a real-shaped catalog after a year without an ontology.

The cost is not aesthetic. A model that has learned what _list means on one tool generalizes it to the next, and a catalog with four naming schemes forces it to learn each tool separately. Selection accuracy degrades gradually and nobody can point at the change that caused it, because there wasn't one.

Two: nobody can say whether the catalog is complete

“Do we cover everything a customer might ask about their account?” is a reasonable question from a product owner and an unanswerable one without a statement of what the catalog ought to cover. In practice the answer is somebody's memory of what has been built, which is how a firm ends up with four ways to read an order and no way to read an execution.

Three: the risk tiers become a matter of taste

This is the expensive one. Each descriptor asserts its own tier. The author of the cancellation tool thinks tier 2; the author of the replacement tool thinks tier 1, because “it's only an amendment”; and two years later an auditor asks why one tool that changes a customer's exposure needs an approval and another does not. There is no good answer, because the two decisions were made in different weeks by different people with no shared rule.

An ontology is not documentation about the catalog. It is the thing the catalog is generated and checked against.

What an ontology fixes, mechanically

All three, and it fixes them by moving decisions from the descriptor to one reviewed file.

Instead ofThe ontology gives you
Choosing a name A name derived from the entity and the operation. A descriptor whose name does not match what the ontology derives fails the build (TS-ONT-02).
Asserting a risk tier A tier that comes from the operation, with every exception recorded as an override and a note that a reviewer reads (TS-ONT-05).
Asserting an approval mode A mode that follows from the tier by a policy table edited in one place (TS-ONT-06).
Hoping the entitlements are right An entitlement floor per entity, so a tool that reads orders cannot be published without the orders scope (TS-ONT-07).
Copying a regex between schemas Identifier types declared once, and a check that every parameter consuming one carries its pattern character for character (TS-ONT-09).
Guessing what the agent needs first A producer graph: which tool hands out each identifier, and therefore what has to happen before what. Chapter 13.
Asking whether the catalog is complete A capability table. Completeness becomes a question about rows rather than about memory, and a declared capability with no descriptor fails the build (TS-SET-02).

The cost

It is one file of about three hundred lines for this catalog, and an afternoon to write the first version. The honest objection is not the cost but the timing: at three tools an ontology is overhead, and at sixty it is a migration. The right moment is somewhere around ten, which is also the moment nobody has time for it.

A cheap way to start. Write the capability table first and nothing else — one row per tool, with the operation, the entity and the identifiers it consumes and produces. That table alone gives you the naming rule, the completeness question and the producer graph. The entities, the policies and the identifier types can follow in a second pass, and each one turns another review argument into a build step.

Chapter 12

Entities, operations, capabilities

Building the brokerage ontology, one decision at a time, with the arguments.

The ontology has five sections and they are worth meeting in the order you would write them: the identifier types that join calls together, the entities the domain is about, the operations that may be performed, the capabilities that pair them, and the policies that follow. 



  
Identifier types


  

    Start here, because identifiers are what make a multi-step conversation possible and what make
    it go wrong.
  


  


  

    Three properties, each load-bearing.
  


  

    They are prefixed. acct_ can never be mistaken for
    ord_, which means a misrouted identifier fails at the schema rather
    than in the handler, and the error names the field.
  


  

    They are opaque, and the ontology says so. Nothing downstream may parse an account
    identifier for a branch code or an open date, because the day somebody does is the day the
    format can never change. The opaque flag is what the generated
    briefing uses to tell the model which identifiers it must never construct.
  


  

    Their patterns are declared once. This is the one that catches real bugs. Look at the
    symbol pattern:
  


  

    
^[A-Z]{1,5}(\.[A-Z])?$

    

      The optional dot-suffix is the whole point. A pattern of
      ^[A-Z]{1,5}$ looks right, passes every test anyone writes, and
      silently rejects BRK.B — which is how a customer discovers
      that your agent cannot price Berkshire Hathaway. Declaring the pattern once means fourteen
      descriptors cannot each get it wrong in their own way.
    

  


  
Entities


  

    An entity is a thing the domain is about. Each one carries the slugs that appear in tool names,
    the identifier that addresses it, the classification floor and the entitlement floor for any
    tool that touches it, and its relations to other entities.
  


  


  

    The brokerage ontology declares nine. Three decisions in that list are worth defending.
  


  
Balances is an entity, not a field on Account

  

    The money view has its own entity because it has its own entitlement
    (brokerage:balances:read), its own freshness and its own tool. A
    service session that may list accounts does not automatically see what is in them; in a firm
    with a support desk that distinction is the difference between a reasonable permission and an
    unreasonable one.
  


  
TaxLot is an entity, not a detail of Position

  

    Because selling is a choice between lots and the choice is worth money. Modeling lots as a
    nested array inside the position payload would have meant that every “what do I
    own?” question paid for a tax breakdown nobody asked for, and that a conversation about
    tax could not happen without one about market value.
  


  
Order is the only mutable entity

  

    "mutable": true appears exactly once in this ontology, and
    everything expensive about the catalog is downstream of that sentence. Four tools touch it,
    three of them change it, all three are tier 2 or above, and the whole of chapter 17 exists
    because of it.
  


  
Operations


  

    A closed vocabulary of seven verbs, each declaring whether it is read-only, whether it is
    idempotent, and what risk tier it starts from.
  


  


  

    The semantics field is prose for humans and it is where the real
    design lives. “Never requires an identifier the agent does not already hold, which is
    what makes a list tool a safe first move” is a design rule, and writing it next to the
    verb means the next person adding a list tool reads it.
  


  
Capabilities


  

    A capability is one row: this tool is this operation on this entity, it needs these identifiers
    and it hands back those. The table is the catalog's specification; the descriptors
    implement it.
  


  


  

    Here are three rows from the brokerage table, chosen because each makes a different point.
  


  


  


  


  

    That last row is how an exception is supposed to look. cancel has a
    base tier of 2 while its siblings have 3, and the note says why in a sentence a reviewer can
    disagree with. An exception with no note is a rule somebody quietly opted out of.
  


  
Policies


  

    Finally, the rules that turn a tier into an approval mode, and the ceiling on what one agent
    turn may do without a human.
  


  


  

    These live in the ontology rather than in each descriptor so that raising the bar —
    moving tier 2 from confirm to
    dualControl, say — is one edit, reviewable in one diff, and
    checked against every descriptor on the next build.
  







  
Chapter 13

  
The producer graph

  

    The one thing an agent needs that no single descriptor can tell it: where the arguments come
    from.
  


  

    A descriptor describes one tool. It can say “accountId, from
    brokerage_accounts_list” in a property description, and it
    should. But that sentence is a claim one author made about another author's tool, and nothing
    checks it. Multiply by fourteen and some of those claims are stale.
  


  

    The producer graph derives the same facts from the capability table, which means they cannot be
    stale, and makes them available as a whole rather than one tool at a time.
  


  
Two maps


  


  


  

    The first says, for each identifier type, which tools hand one out. The second inverts it: for
    each tool, which tools must have run first because they are the only source of something it
    needs. Both are computed, not written.
  


  
What it says about this catalog

Dead ends


  

    The graph also answers a question nobody thinks to ask: is there an identifier some tool needs
    that nothing in the catalog produces?
  


  


  

    Every one is a tool the agent can read about and can never correctly call, because the argument
    has to come from the customer typing an opaque identifier exactly right. It happens more than
    you would expect — usually when a tool is ported from an internal API where the caller
    already had the identifier from somewhere else. The linter makes it a build failure
    (TS-SET-03).

Chapter 14

  
Conformance against the ontology

  

    Nine rules that turn a design document into a build step.
  


  

    An ontology that nothing checks is a diagram on a wiki. These are the rules that make it bind,
    and each one exists because of a specific way a catalog drifts from its own design.
  


  

    
RuleChecks
      The drift it catches

    
      
TS-ONT-01Every descriptor has a capability
        A tool built in a hurry that the ontology has never heard of — which usually means
          it is doing something the domain was not designed to do.

      
TS-ONT-02The name matches what the ontology derives
        A rename, a plural that should be singular, a verb in the wrong position.

      
TS-ONT-03The capability's entity and operation exist
        A typo in the ontology itself.

      
TS-ONT-04readOnlyHint agrees with the
        operation
        A write tool annotated read-only, which is the single most consequential annotation
          error available.

      
TS-ONT-05The risk tier matches the policy
        A tool quietly demoted to avoid an approval requirement.

      
TS-ONT-06The approval mode matches the tier
        A tier 3 tool with mode: none, usually left over from
          development.

      
TS-ONT-07The entity's entitlement is held
        A reader of orders published without the orders scope, visible to every session.

      
TS-ONT-08The classification is not below the entity's
        Customer data published as internal, which changes who may
          see the logs.

      
TS-ONT-09Every consumed identifier's pattern is carried
        A looser pattern on one tool than on its neighbors — how a malformed identifier
          reaches a handler.

    And the pattern check, which is the one that catches the most bugs per line:
  


  


  
Rules about the set, not the tool


  

    Three more rules look at the catalog as a whole, which is where the interesting gaps are.
  


  


  

    TS-SET-01 catches two tools sharing a title. 
    TS-SET-02 catches a capability the ontology declares and nothing implements — which
    is the completeness question, asked automatically. TS-SET-03 catches
    the dead ends from chapter 13. And TS-SET-04 warns when most of a
    catalog changes state, because a model needs reads to orient itself before it is safe to let it
    write.
  


  
It fails at startup, not at call time


  

    The registry runs the linter in its constructor and refuses to build if anything is in error.
  


  


  

    A non-conformant descriptor therefore cannot reach a model. Not “should not”;
    cannot — the application does not start. The same rules also run as an ordinary unit test,
    which is what fails the build for everyone, and as a command, which gives whoever is writing a
    descriptor a one-second loop instead of a Maven cycle.
  


  

    Why both. The test is the gate. The command is the ergonomics. A gate
    with bad ergonomics gets worked around, and a gate that is worked around is a style guide.
