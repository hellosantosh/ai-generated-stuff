Front matter

How to read this book

It is a specification with a worked implementation attached, which means there are four sensible routes through it and only one of them is cover to cover.

The book is about one question: how do you give a language model the ability to act on a brokerage account without eventually regretting it? Everything here is an answer to some part of that. The descriptor is how you tell the model what a tool does. The ontology is how you stop fourteen descriptors drifting apart. The registry and the runtime are how you make a confident wrong answer harmless. The harness is how you find out whether any of it worked before a customer does.

Four routes

If you areRead
Writing your first tool next week Chapter 4 for the shape of a descriptor, chapter 6 for the description, chapter 15 for one tool built end to end, then chapter 21 for what has to happen around it. About forty pages, and enough to ship something defensible.
Reviewing somebody else's catalog Appendix D is the checklist. Chapter 35 is how to run the meeting. Chapters 9 and 10 are what the governance block has to say, and chapter 37 is the list of things that have actually gone wrong for other people.
Responsible for the platform Parts III, V and VII — the ontology, the registry and the runtime, and the testing. These are the three investments that stop a catalog decaying as it grows, and all three are cheaper to make at ten tools than at sixty.
Here for the agent Part VI. Chapter 25 is what a model is actually reading when it chooses; chapter 27 walks five real conversations tool by tool, including two where the agent is wrong and one where it is right to refuse.

What is in the repository

The companion repository is a working system, not a set of snippets: fourteen conformant descriptors, a brokerage behind them, a registry, a ten-step invocation pipeline, a Spring AI agent over Claude, an Angular console, a desktop connector and an evaluation harness with thirty scenarios. It builds and tests in about fifteen seconds with no API key and no network.

How the code in these pages got here

Every code figure in this book is an excerpt pulled from a file in the repository at build time, by a directive that names the file and the first and last lines to take. Every figure that shows output — an envelope, an error, a confirmation dialog, the generated system prompt, the conformance report, a run of the harness — was recorded from the running system by a capture script and read from disk by the same build.

Nothing is retyped, and that is not a flourish. A specification book whose examples have drifted from its reference implementation is worse than no book, because the reader cannot tell which half to trust. Here, a figure that disagrees with the code is a build failure.

A note on what this book does not cover. It names no wire protocol. The descriptor is a JSON document and publishing it to a particular tool-calling runtime is an adapter — the repository has two, and each is one class. Everything that matters about a tool is in the contract, which is why the contract is what gets specified, linted, reviewed, versioned and tested.

The running example

One customer, four accounts, and a book of business arranged so that the easy design decisions fail on it.

Every example in this book runs against the same demonstration customer. The accounts are deliberately awkward, because a catalog tested against a single healthy margin account will do the wrong thing on three accounts out of four and nobody will notice until a customer calls. 



  

    Four accounts, and each one breaks something. The margin account can borrow and sell
    short, so its buying power is not its cash. The cash account can do neither, and its
    buying power is capped by settled cash, which is a different number again. The
    Roth IRA cannot sell short at all, and inside it every sentence about holding periods
    and wash sales is irrelevant. The rollover IRA is frozen, which means an agent that
    helpfully tries the customer's other account has just done something nobody asked for.
  


  
What is in the margin account


  


  

    Five holdings, one of them an index fund that is 31 percent of the account — concentrated
    by the numbers and not by any sensible reading, which is the sort of thing an agent should say
    out loud rather than flag mechanically. Two holdings sit just under the 25 percent line.
    One, GME, is down a third and is not marginable, so buying more of it
    needs settled cash even here.
  


  
And the money


  


  

    This is the single most instructive payload in the book. A customer asking “how much have
    I got?” means one of four things and never says which. cash is
    the ledger figure. settledCash is what can be spent today without
    risking a good-faith violation. buyingPower is what can actually be
    spent on a purchase right now, net of the margin loan and of the
    cashReservedForOrders already committed to working orders.
    totalEquity is what the statement will say.
  

  

    They differ here by a factor of three. A tool that returned one number called
    balance would be correct a quarter of the time, and the three
    quarters where it is wrong include every question that ends in somebody spending money.
  


  

    
The conversation this book is about

    

      “Can you sell some of my Apple and put it into the index fund? Actually — how much
      would that even cost me in tax? And don't do anything in the retirement one.”
    

    

      Four accounts to choose between, two companies whose names contain the word Apple, a holding
      split across a long-term lot and a short-term lot bought six weeks ago, a question about tax
      that must be answered with facts and not advice, and an instruction to stay out of an account
      that is frozen anyway. Everything in the next four hundred pages exists so that an agent
      handles that sentence the way a good human would: by asking two questions, showing its
      arithmetic, and not trading until somebody says yes.
